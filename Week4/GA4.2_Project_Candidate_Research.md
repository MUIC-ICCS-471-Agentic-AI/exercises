# GA4.2 — Explore Possible Term Projects

## Assignment Type
Team assignment · **20 points**

## Learning Objectives
Understand a software problem, identify the people affected, and explore a useful project your team could build.

## Team Formation
Form teams of three. One team may have four members, giving us four teams in total. Confirm your team ID (T01–T04) and members with the instructor.

## Duration
Start in class and continue outside class as needed. Allow roughly 90–120 minutes for an initial investigation. Submit by the Classroom deadline.

## Task Description
Explore **two or three possible term projects** and choose the one your team currently prefers. Two thoughtfully explored ideas are enough for full marks.

Create a Google Doc with a clear document title, a separate section titled with each candidate project’s name, and numbered subheadings for the **10 questions below**. Answer in short paragraphs or bullets. A few sentences per question are enough. This is an early exploration: your ideas can change as you learn more. You are researching possibilities this week; implementation comes later.

### A. Explore each candidate

1. **Context:** Where would this project be used? Describe the setting, such as a student club, small business, community or everyday activity.
2. **Problem statement:** What specific problem would the project address, and why is it worth solving?
3. **Stakeholders and beneficiaries:** Who would use it, benefit from it, or be affected by it? Briefly explain their roles.
4. **Current approach:** How do people handle this task now? Describe an existing tool or process.
5. **Pain points and needs:** What is frustrating, slow or difficult about the current approach? What would the people involved need from a better solution?
6. **Main features:** What **two or three main features** would you initially plan to implement? Explain how they address the needs you identified.
7. **Research:** Where have you looked, and what did you learn? Refer to relevant websites, existing applications, articles or documentation. Distinguish what you found from what your team is still assuming.
8. **Feasibility:** What tools or technologies might you use? Is this manageable within the course, and what is the main difficulty or uncertainty you would need to investigate?
9. **Use of AI during development:** How might Copilot help your team build this project, and what would you check yourselves? The application itself does not need an AI feature.
10. **Success:** How would you check that the software works correctly and helps its intended users? Give one simple example of correct behaviour and say who could give useful feedback.

> **Needs and features are related, but different.**
>
> Need: A club organiser needs to know who still has borrowed equipment.
>
> Possible feature: A list of current loans showing the borrower and expected return date.

### B. Sources and citations

Use **one or two relevant sources per candidate** that your team has actually read or explored. A source may support more than one candidate. Your own experience can also inform the idea; describe it as your observation.

Use **APA-style in-text citations** where you refer to a source, and give the full references at the end. For a typical webpage:

> **Citation format**
>
> In the answer: `(Author or Organisation, Year)`
>
> In the reference list: Author or Organisation. (Year, Month Day). *Title of webpage*. Website Name. URL
>
> Use `n.d.` when no publication date is available. Omit the website name if it is the same as the author. Replace the placeholders with the source's actual details.

For more examples, see the [University of Nevada, Reno APA webpage citation guide](https://library.unr.edu/help/quick-how-tos/citing/citing-a-website-in-apa).

### C. Choose a direction

After exploring the candidates, write **one short paragraph** explaining which project your team prefers and why. Consider its usefulness, your team's interest and whether you can realistically build it. Mention one question you still want to investigate.

This choice is a starting point for discussion with the instructor and your later project proposal.

## AI Policy and Team Contribution
AI may help you brainstorm ideas, find search terms and organise your thoughts. Read the sources yourselves and write the answers and recommendation in your team's own words. Briefly state which AI tools you used and how.

Everyone should contribute to the research and discussion. Add one sentence per member describing their contribution. **Do not plagiarise.** Cite ideas and information borrowed from others.

## Submission Requirements
Export your Google Doc as a PDF. One member submits it to Classroom for the team, named `ICCS471_GA4.2_TeamXX.pdf` (for example, `ICCS471_GA4.2_Team01.pdf`). Use clear titles and section headings throughout. Include:

- Team ID and all members' names/student IDs.
- Answers to the 10 questions for each of your two or three candidates.
- Your preferred project and reasons.
- APA-style references, member contributions and a brief AI declaration.

## Rubric
**Total: 20 points. Each criterion is worth 4 points.**

| Criterion | What your work should show | Points |
|---|---|---|
| Context, problem and stakeholders | Each candidate has an understandable setting, a specific problem and relevant stakeholders or beneficiaries. | 4 |
| Needs and proposed features | The current approach and pain points are explained; two or three features respond to the identified needs. | 4 |
| Research and sources | Relevant sources inform the ideas, with APA-style citations and references that readers can follow. | 4 |
| Feasibility and development approach | Sensible initial thoughts about feasibility, AI assistance, checks and user feedback. | 4 |
| Recommendation and teamwork | A reasoned choice between candidates and a brief account of each member's contribution. | 4 |

For each criterion: **4** = clear and well supported; **3** = mostly clear, with a minor gap; **2** = partly developed; **1** = very limited; **0** = missing or not assessable. Clear thinking and relevant research matter more than length. Minor citation-formatting errors alone do not prevent full marks when the sources are clear and traceable.
